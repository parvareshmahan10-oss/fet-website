The Story Behind FET

A 2,500 Year
Question

Why do people differ so consistently in their behaviour? The search for an answer spans millennia — from ancient medicine to modern neuroscience.

Scroll to explore
01
Part One

From Hippocrates
to Neuroscience

The study of consistent behavioural patterns is not new. Over 2,500 years ago, Hippocrates and Galen described four temperament types — choleric, melancholic, phlegmatic, and sanguine — attributing them to imbalances in bodily fluids.

What is remarkable is that modern psychiatric classifications still echo these ancient categories. ADHD resembles the choleric type; depression echoes melancholia; autism spectrum mirrors the phlegmatic; mania reflects the sanguine.

The ancient intuition was right: consistent behavioural patterns have a biological basis. Modern neurochemistry has confirmed that imbalances in neurotransmitter systems can induce the same patterns Hippocrates described.

~400 BC
Hippocrates & Galen
Four temperament types linked to chemical imbalances in the body. First systematic theory of behavioural differences.
1906–1935
Ivan Pavlov
Experimental psychophysiology tradition begins. Identified CNS strength, mobility, and balance as foundations of individual differences.
1948–1970
Alexander Luria
Three functional brain blocks: energetic arousal, programming, and information processing. A neurological framework for behaviour.
1967
Hans Eysenck
Proposed General Arousal as the basis of Extraversion. Influential but ultimately too simple — one dial cannot explain the complexity of temperament.
1982
Jeffrey Gray
BAS and BIS — two motivational systems. Closer to the truth, but still insufficient to capture the full neurochemical picture.
1989
Vladimir Rusalov
Activity-specific model: separates physical, social, and mental aspects of temperament. First model to distinguish context of behaviour.
02
Part Two

Why Existing
Models Fall Short

Most personality models in use today — MBTI, Big Five, and others — were built from a fundamental methodological flaw: they started from language, not biology.

The lexical approach assumes that if a trait matters, people will have words for it. But language is shaped by culture, social expectations, and communication needs — not by neurotransmitter function.

The result is models that describe what you do, but cannot tell you why. They offer labels, not mechanisms. And without mechanisms, they cannot predict how temperament relates to mental health, treatment response, or biological variation.

MBTI
1940s
Based on Jungian typology adapted for popular use. No neurochemical grounding, poor test-retest reliability, and binary categories that fail to capture continuous variation.
No biological basis
Big Five
1980s–90s
Derived from factor analysis of language. Mixes biological and cultural traits — Agreeableness conflates Empathy (neurochemical) with Politeness (cultural). Cannot explain psychopathology.
Language-based taxonomy
Eysenck
1967
General Arousal theory reduces all individual differences to a single dial. Cannot explain why someone can have high mental endurance but low motor endurance simultaneously.
One-dimensional arousal
Gray RST
1982
Two systems (BAS/BIS) are an improvement but insufficient. Fails to explain GAD patients who show simultaneously high anxiety and high impulsivity — contradicting the model's predictions.
Two systems insufficient
03
Part Three

How FET
Was Found

The Functional Ensemble of Temperament did not emerge from a single insight. It was built through decades of convergent evidence — from neuropsychology, psychopharmacology, and differential psychology — all pointing toward the same conclusion.

The key insight: three neurotransmitter systems independently regulate three universal aspects of all behaviour — its orientation, integration, and maintenance. This was not a theoretical invention, but a discovery hiding in plain sight across multiple research traditions.

When Dr. Irina Trofimova mapped Rusalov's activity-specific model onto neurotransmitter function, and when Prof. Trevor Robbins confirmed the neurochemical specificity from Cambridge's pharmacological work, the framework crystallised into FET.

P
Pavlov tradition · Russia · 1906
The Psychophysiological Foundation
Experimental tradition measuring CNS properties through EEG, evoked potentials, and reaction time tasks in healthy adults.
R
Vladimir Rusalov · Moscow · 1989
Activity-Specific Temperament
First model separating physical, social-verbal, and mental aspects of behaviour. Measured endurance, tempo, and plasticity in each domain independently.
T
Irina Trofimova · McMaster · 2007–2018
Neurochemical Mapping
Rusalov's PhD student revised the STQ model by mapping each temperament dimension to specific neurotransmitter ensembles, informed by thousands of pharmacological studies.
Rb
Trevor Robbins · Cambridge · 2016
Neurochemical Confirmation
Director of the Behavioural and Clinical Neuroscience Institute at Cambridge provided independent neurochemical evidence confirming the functional specialisation of NA, DA, and ACh/5-HT systems.
F
Trofimova & Robbins · 2016
FET — The Integrated Framework
The convergence of psychophysiology, neuropsychology, and pharmacology produced FET: 12 temperament traits organised in a 3×4 matrix, each anchored to specific neurochemical ensembles.
